About

I am an architect and urban designer. I read a plan for two things at once: who moves through a space, and who gets left at its edge.

The question began in a neighborhood clinic I grew up beside. It treated fevers and gave injections, but it was also where the block gathered: parents talked while they waited, a television played in the injection room, a neighbor kept an eye on someone else's child. Years later, at a drafting desk, I watched a corridor meant for families to rest get cut so the beds could move faster. The same trade-off, at two scales.

So I follow one line. A clinic can stand a short walk away and still be out of reach, and it is the space in between, the streets and crossings and whoever is there to walk them with you, that decides. I want to read that space closely, and to say plainly who it reaches and who it leaves.

Asking

As neighborhood clinics vanish, cities keep assuming that a facility near enough will be used. My work asks what actually decides whether people reach the care nearest them, and for whom the answer changes.

  1. Under what conditions does the spatial structure around a neighborhood care facility decide whether it becomes routine daily use or an isolated destination?
  2. For whom does that difference matter most, and how does social isolation change the answer?
  3. Can street-network structure, not distance, predict whether people actually reach the care nearest them?
