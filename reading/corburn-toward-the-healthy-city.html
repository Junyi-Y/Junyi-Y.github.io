Reading note

Toward the Healthy City

Jason Corburn · reading note

The argument. Corburn rejects the physical determinism that has dominated planning and argues for a relational view: healthy places are doubly constructed, through physical form and through social interaction, meaning, and political institutions. He documents tools that link land use and infrastructure to health outcomes (Health Impact Assessment, the Healthy Development Measurement Tool) and recovers the early-twentieth-century Neighborhood Health Centers that combined clinical care with community participation, New York's milk stations with visiting nurses, block committees, physicians matched to a community's culture.

For my research. It keeps me from reading space as destiny. It gives me a way to translate spatial loss into the social determinants of health, and into embodiment and the weathering effect: the chronic stress of lost access and social support that accumulates as biological damage, most of all for vulnerable groups. And it gives me a historical precedent for the dual clinical-and-social node I argue for.

The gap it leaves. How to design neighborhood health infrastructure that integrates clinical and social functions without reproducing the exclusionary zoning the twentieth-century neighborhood unit became. Equity has to be built into the model, not assumed.

Source · Toward the Healthy City (MIT Press, 2009) · Jason Corburn
