Asking

Proximity is not access.

A neighborhood care facility can sit within a fifteen-minute walk and still go unused. What decides whether people reach it is the space in between: the street structure between them, and whether anyone is there to make the trip. I study that space, and for whom it turns out to be decisive.

The question is pressing because the ground is shifting. Across aging cities, care is consolidating into large hospitals while the small clinics and day centers that once sat inside neighborhoods disappear. Two policy stances show how differently cities read the loss. New York's NORC model places services where older residents have already concentrated, reacting to need; Shanghai's fifteen-minute community life circle embeds care into the street network by design. Both still assume nearness is enough. My own field work keeps contradicting that assumption. Along a Bangkok canal I measured heat and watched who crossed a street for shade or a temple courtyard and who would not, depending on the route and the company. Nearness was never the whole story.

The gap

Three fields circle this problem without closing on it. Healthy-neighborhood research measures distance to facilities but rarely asks whether nearness becomes routine use. Health-and-aging research, including the age-friendly-communities work of Andy Hong's HARP Lab, is strong on neighborhood-scale data but stops short of the individual facility and the streets around it. Ann Forsyth's survey of the healthy-city field names the blind spot directly: neighborhood healthcare infrastructure is seldom treated as a spatial system inside everyday life. Eric Klinenberg and Jason Corburn establish that social infrastructure and neighborhood health nodes shape health, but largely through narrative and history rather than spatial measurement. The measurable question, whether the layout between a person and a facility predicts who arrives, is still open.

The question

Under what conditions does the spatial structure around a neighborhood care facility decide whether it becomes part of daily life or an isolated destination, and for whom does that difference matter most?

The argument

Neighborhood health infrastructure works as a contingent system: the facility and the street structure around it function together, and the strength of that connection depends on who lives nearby. Street-level connectivity becomes more consequential as social isolation rises. A caregiving network can absorb a difficult route; its absence makes the route decisive. The same clinic, at the same distance, is within reach for one household and out of reach for the one next door.

Methods

I test this through a comparative case study of New York and Shanghai, two cities with opposite proximity assumptions. Using OSMnx street networks and Space Syntax, with facility and population data, I measure pedestrian catchment through betweenness centrality, local integration, network distance, and route directness, focused on neighborhoods with high single-elderly density. The hypothesis is specific: route directness, not straight-line distance, predicts routine use, and the gap between the two widens for people aging alone. The approach extends work I have already done, mapping vulnerability and access across St. Louis, and collecting the household-composition data behind a citywide aging-care plan in Xiangyang.

Contribution

Answering this changes two things. In practice, it argues for pairing where care is sited with an audit of the streets around it, so investment reaches the neighborhoods where the layout, not the distance, is failing single-elderly households. In theory, it gives the disappearance of neighborhood health nodes a measurable consequence, connecting spatial loss to the social determinants of health and the weathering effect that Corburn describes, and it treats the third places Klinenberg documents as health infrastructure that can be mapped and tested. It adds a spatial-structural dimension to what neighborhood-effects research calls contextual influence.

Foundations

The reading the argument is built on. Each links to my full annotation.

  • Jason Corburn, Toward the Healthy City. A relational view of place, and a way to turn spatial loss into a measurable health consequence.
  • Eric Klinenberg, Palaces for the People. Third places as social infrastructure, reframed here as health infrastructure to be measured.
  • Ann Forsyth, What is a Healthy Place? The field's map of healthy places, and the neighborhood healthcare infrastructure it leaves out, which is where I work.
  • Andy Hong, on age-friendly communities. My primary anchor: aging and the built environment as one question, strong on the neighborhood, which my work carries down to the facility.
  • Valerie Greer, on aging in place. Where the family-support moderator in my argument comes from.
