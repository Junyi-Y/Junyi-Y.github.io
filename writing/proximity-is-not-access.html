Proximity is not access: NYC and Shanghai

Abstract accepted · Urban Transitions Conference · 2026

For older adults, a clinic within walking distance does not guarantee access to care. A fifteen-minute walk can become impassable when the route crosses an arterial road or passes through mixed pedestrian-vehicle traffic. The barrier is not distance: it is the urban environment between the person and the facility. Proximity does not equal access.

Three fields approach this problem from different angles. Healthy-neighborhood research measures distance to facilities but seldom asks whether proximity becomes routine use. Health-and-aging research tracks how neighborhoods shape population health but rarely examines how the spatial structure between a facility and its streets determines who walks through the door. Architecture-and-health scholarship has focused on building interiors, leaving the urban scale unexamined.

I argue that neighborhood health infrastructure works as a contingent system: the care facility and the urban spatial structure around it function together, and the strength of that connection depends on who lives nearby. This builds on what neighborhood-effects research calls the “contextual influence” by adding a spatial-structural dimension. Street-level connectivity becomes more consequential as social isolation increases: caregiving networks reduce its impact, but their absence makes it determinative.

This paper tests this argument through a comparative case study in New York and Shanghai, two cities with opposite proximity assumptions. Shanghai's fifteen-minute community life circle embeds healthcare proactively into the street network; New York's NORC model allocates services reactively, where older adults have already concentrated. Both assume nearness is sufficient. Using OSMnx street networks, NYC DOH facility records, and Gaode POI data, we measure pedestrian catchment using four metrics: betweenness centrality, local integration, origin-to-facility network distance, and route directness ratio. We hypothesize that the route directness ratio predicts routine-use rates more strongly than distance, especially for older adults aging alone. Findings suggest pairing facility siting with street-network audits in neighborhoods with high single-elderly density.
