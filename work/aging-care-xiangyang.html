Matching a citywide network of care facilities to where the need actually is.

Xiangyang Urban Planning and Design Research Institute, Hubei, China · Summer 2018 · research assistant.

This project translated China's national aging-care policy into a facility plan for Xiangyang. Working in the research phase, I surveyed the city's older residents, then used that evidence to decide where care should be kept, renovated, expanded, or newly built. Three care models, home-based, community-based, and institutional, were mapped onto a three-tier spatial hierarchy of district, residential-neighborhood, and community.

The problem

The city's care facilities sat where they were easy to build, not where older residents lived. Profiling residents by income, household composition, and health made one thing clear: the same proximity means something different for different people. That turned a siting exercise into a question about who actually reaches the facilities a city builds.

Method

Through field observation, interviews, and questionnaires, I profiled the city's older residents by household composition, income and its sources, living and health conditions, and their care needs and preferences.

Analysis of current conditions

Most older adults remain physically independent, yet their concerns run past medical dependency: future caregiving, mobility loss, financial burden, and social isolation. Many have adult children in the same city or neighborhood, but family proximity does not always become daily support, and uneven visitation points to uneven care. The clear preference was for home-based and community-supported aging, staying in familiar surroundings while keeping access to daily help.

From demand to a service network

Demand is not aimed at institutional beds. It centers on home and community support, flowing across a layered system of in-home service stations, community day-care, short-term respite, and other community facilities. The planning implication is a shift from a bed-supply approach to a service-network one.

A planning framework

A framework carries the survey findings into spatial strategy. Care needs and preferences are validated against a health-gradient model and the 90-7-3 care ratio, then translated into a facility system and its outputs: a siting strategy, 15-minute aging-care living circles, area and bed indicators, and age-friendly design guidelines.

The plan

The plan redistributes facilities so that care falls within a 15-minute walk, tier by tier, distinguishing which to retain, renovate, or build new.

Result

At the smallest scale, a community facility is designed not as an isolated institution but as a node in a neighborhood support network for everyday health, social contact, and active aging.

In my research

Family composition, the variable that moderates isolation and access in my dissertation, is the same variable I measured here, at citywide scale and in the country my comparative case includes. The finding that nearby family does not guarantee support is where proximity is not access began.
