Measuring microclimate, and watching who can actually use a space.

Urban Design Research Theories and Methods, with Bomin Kim · Bangkok · 2023.

A field study of microclimate and public space along a Bangkok canal. I measured air and surface temperatures across shaded, sun-exposed, and planted areas, and paired each reading with observation of how residents actually used the space in the heat. The finding was consistent: nearness to a cool or shaded spot was not the same as being able to use it.

The problem

In a hot, dense city, whether an outdoor space can be used is a health question, not a question of comfort. Distance to a plaza or a park says little about whether people can bear to be in it. The study asked what actually makes a public space usable in the heat, and for whom.

Method

I measured air and surface temperatures across three conditions, shaded, sun-exposed, and planted, at sites along the canal, and recorded how people used each space through the day. Alongside the field survey, I mapped the district's street network and figure-ground to read how the built fabric structures access to cool space.

Findings

The observations carried the argument. People gathered under a store awning for the one patch of shade on a concrete street. A temple worked as the neighborhood's community center and midday refuge. By the water, residents slept in a breeze-cooled pavilion while the iron benches beside them stayed too hot to sit on. Comfort was the product of built form, planting, and wind together, and small differences decided whether a space was used at all.

Result

The field study became a design: a proposed network of shaded, planted, water-linked public spaces along the canal, so that cool space is not left to chance.

In my research

This is the same move my dissertation makes at a larger scale. Being near a space is not the same as being able to use it.
