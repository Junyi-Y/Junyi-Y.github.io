Spatial equity mapping: heat, vulnerability, and access.

Washington University in St. Louis, Radical Mapping with Prof. Patty Heyda · Spring 2024 · individual project.

This project mapped how extreme-heat vulnerability is distributed across St. Louis, where the urban heat island overlaps with older, low-income, and mobility-limited residents. Working at the community scale in GIS, I layered environmental and social data, down to the gap between planned and surviving tree canopy and the operating hours of cooling centers, to make compounding vulnerability legible.

The argument

The maps establish that vulnerability is not a property of individuals but a geography, produced where environmental exposure, physical access, and institutional availability overlap. Reading them is where my question first formed: for whom does access matter most.

Where heat concentrates

The first two maps set the exposure. Heat is worst in dense, paved districts with little canopy, and the residents least able to cope are clustered on the same ground.

Where proximity fails

The next three maps zoom into one of the most vulnerable communities and test whether nearby care is usable care. It rarely is.

In my research

Reading public buildings as health infrastructure, and vulnerability as overlapping geographies rather than one number, is the direct precursor to my dissertation question about for whom access matters most. The bus stops I documented, most of them fully exposed to the sun, became a first measure of a route that is near but unusable.
